Discovery and Initial Misdiagnosis

1998 – 1999 · Peninsular Malaysia

The first outbreak

September 1998 – April 1999 — the first recorded Nipah virus (NiV) outbreak in humans occurred in Peninsular Malaysia. The epicenter was later traced to a pig farm in Ipoh, Perak, where the first human case had already appeared in January 1997.

The disease presented as a cluster of severe, acute febrile encephalitis among pig farmers near Ipoh. Because Japanese encephalitis (JE) was endemic to the region, the outbreak was initially misdiagnosed as JE. Authorities launched mass JE vaccination and mosquito control, but the outbreak expanded south to Sikamat (Negeri Sembilan) in December 1998, culminating in the largest cluster in Bukit Pelandok.

  • Epidemiological clues ruled out JE: most patients were adult males with occupational pig contact (JE typically affects children), and vaccinated individuals still fell ill.
  • Farmers reported pigs with severe respiratory illness and a loud, barking cough (“barking pig syndrome”).
  • In March 1999, a novel paramyxovirus was isolated from CSF of a patient from Kampung Sungai Nipah (Nipah River Village) – giving the virus its name.
First case: Jan 1997 Ipoh, Perak NiV-M strain

Bats → Pigs → Humans

Spillover: Pig farms in Perak were situated near commercial fruit orchards (mango, etc.) that attracted wild fruit bats (Pteropus species), the natural NiV reservoir. Bats fed on fruit and dropped partially eaten pieces contaminated with saliva, urine, or faeces into pig pens, introducing the virus to a naïve pig population.

Amplification in pigs: Pigs acted as highly efficient amplifying hosts. The virus spread via respiratory secretions and direct contact. Infected pigs developed laboured breathing and a harsh, non-productive cough – “barking pig syndrome” or “one‑mile cough”. Morbidity in confined swine reached nearly 100%, but mortality was low (1–5%).

Spillover to humans: Direct contact with infected pigs or raw secretions (saliva, urine, tissue) during farming, slaughtering or handling was the primary risk. In a case‑control study, 92% of infected patients had direct contact with pigs.

No sustained human‑to‑human transmission — unlike later outbreaks in Bangladesh and India, interhuman spread was extremely rare in Malaysia.

Epidemiology & clinical features

  • Cases & deaths: 265 lab‑confirmed cases, 105 deaths (CFR 39.6%). Some reviews cite up to 283 symptomatic cases and 109 deaths.
  • Demographic: Vast majority were adult males with occupational livestock exposure.
  • Clinical presentation: Rapid progressive encephalitic syndrome: high fever, severe headache, dizziness, myalgia, then altered mental status, confusion, areflexia, seizures, segmental myoclonus, nystagmus, and coma.
  • Lineage: NiV‑Malaysia (NiV‑M) strain. Notably, human patients lacked severe respiratory symptoms (atypical pneumonia or ARDS), contrasting with the NiV‑B strain in Bangladesh and India.

Spread to Singapore & containment

In March 1999, the outbreak reached Singapore, causing 11 cases and 1 death among abattoir workers handling live pigs imported from Malaysia.

  • Mass swine culling: Malaysian authorities culled over 1 million pigs in affected regions; carcasses buried deep and decontaminated with quicklime.
  • Import bans & farm closures: Singapore banned live pig imports from Malaysia and shut local abattoirs. Malaysia banned inter‑state pig transport and launched national surveillance.
  • Domestic animal culling: Infected dogs on farms were also culled to prevent further transmission.
  • Economic impact: The culling devastated the livestock industry, with estimated losses of $350–400 million for Malaysia.
  • The last confirmed human case of this outbreak was reported in May 1999. No new NiV outbreaks have been reported in Malaysia since.

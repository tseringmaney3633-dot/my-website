Genomic and Protein Composition

The genome of the Nipah virus is single-stranded negative-sense RNA, approximately 18.2 kb in length, which makes it (along with Hendra virus) exceptionally larger than typical paramyxoviruses

The genome is consecutively organized into six structural genes in the order of 3'\text{-N-P-M-F-G-L-}5', which encode the virus's primary proteins

  1. Nucleocapsid Protein (N): This protein binds directly to the viral RNA to form the viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) core. It is responsible for governing viral RNA production and regulating viral polymerase activity.

  2. Phosphoprotein (P): Forms part of the vRNP complex. The P gene is also highly complex, serving as the template for multiple crucial non-structural proteins.

  3. Matrix Protein (M): Shapes the inner viral shell beneath the envelope.It acts as an organizer that coordinates viral assembly, morphogenesis, and the budding of new virions from host cells.

  4. Fusion Protein (F): An enveloped glycoprotein that drives host cell entry. It triggers the critical membrane-fusion process between the host cell and the viral envelope. On infected cell surfaces, the expression of F causes neighboring cells to fuse, forming multinucleated giant cells (syncytia)—a classic histopathological hallmark of NiV infection.

  5. Attachment Glycoprotein (G): Another major enveloped surface protein.It is responsible for recognizing and binding to host receptors, specifically Ephrin-B2 and Ephrin-B3.Antibodies directed against the G protein are key to neutralizing viral infectivity.

  6. Long Polymerase (L): The large viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase enzyme responsible for synthesizing viral mRNA and replicating the viral genome.

Accessory and Non-Structural Proteins (C, V, W)

Using a co-transcriptional gene editing (stuttering) mechanism and alternative start codons, the P gene also expresses three accessory proteins that are not incorporated into the physical virion but play critical roles during infection

  1. V and W Proteins: These are master immune-evasion proteins.They block mammalian host innate immunity by directly binding to cellular signaling proteins (like STAT1, STAT2, MDA5, and RIG-I), preventing the cell from producing or responding to type I interferons (alpha/beta).

  2. C Protein: This protein typically resides in the cytoplasm of infected host cells, where it regulates viral RNA synthesis, mediates virion release, and serves as a key factor in overall viral virulence.

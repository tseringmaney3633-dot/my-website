🔬 Overview of Host Range

Why NiV infects so many different mammals

Nipah virus (NiV) is characterized by an exceptionally broad host range, with documented natural infections, serological exposures, or experimental susceptibilities spanning at least six distinct mammalian orders. This wide tropism is highly unusual for paramyxoviruses, which typically have a narrow, species-restricted host range.

🧬 Biological Driver: This broad cross-species compatibility is biologically driven by the virus's utilization of the highly conserved host cell surface receptors, Ephrin-B2 and Ephrin-B3, for cell entry. Because Ephrin-B2 plays a critical role in cellular migration and vasculogenesis, it is evolutionarily conserved across a vast array of mammals, with receptor sequence similarity among bats, pigs, and humans approaching 95% to 96%.

🦇 1. Order Chiroptera (Bats)

Primary natural hosts and reservoirs

Members of the order Chiroptera serve as the primary natural hosts and reservoirs of the virus. They are infected without showing significant clinical symptoms or pathological changes, allowing persistent viral circulation within bat populations.

🦇
Genus Pteropus (Flying Foxes)

The established primary natural reservoir. Outbreaks closely align with Pteropus distribution.

  • Pteropus giganteus India & Bangladesh
  • Pteropus vampyrus Malaysia, Indonesia, East Timor
  • Pteropus hypomelanus Malaysia
  • Pteropus lylei Cambodia & Thailand
🦇
Other Bat Genera

Non-Pteropid fruit bats also show evidence of exposure.

  • Rousettus amplexicaudatus Non-Pteropid fruit bat; positive for NiV RNA in East Timor
  • Eidolon helvum Straw-colored fruit bat; henipavirus exposure in Ghana, West Africa

🐒 2. Order Primates (Humans & Non-Human Primates)

Highly susceptible, often severe disease

Primates are highly susceptible to Nipah virus infection, which often manifests as severe, fatal neurological and respiratory disease.

👤
Humans (Homo sapiens)

Highly affected, exhibiting:

  • Encephalitis Rapid, progressive brain inflammation
  • Respiratory Atypical pneumonia, ARDS
  • CFR 40-75% or more
  • Sequelae Permanent neurological deficits
🐵
Non-Human Primates

Used as laboratory models for vaccines and therapeutics.

  • African Green Monkeys Premier model; severe interstitial pneumonia, fatal encephalitis
  • Squirrel Monkeys Develop symptomatic infection
  • Cynomolgus Macaques Notably do NOT develop symptomatic infection

🐖 3. Order Artiodactyla (Even-Toed Ungulates)

Critical intermediate hosts and vectors

🐷
Pigs (Sus scrofa)

Critical intermediate and amplifying hosts in Malaysia/Singapore (1998-1999).

  • Disease: Porcine Respiratory & Encephalitis Syndrome (PRES)
  • Signs: "Barking pig syndrome" or "one-mile cough"
  • Morbidity: ~100% in enclosed populations
  • Mortality: Low (1-5%), except in young piglets
🐄
Other Livestock (Cattle, Goats, Sheep)

Susceptible with varying degrees of disease.

  • Cattle Serological evidence in Bangladesh; linked to human transmission
  • Goats High seroprevalence in endemic areas
  • Sheep Virus infects monocytes, NK cells, T-lymphocytes

🐴 4. Order Perissodactyla (Odd-Toed Ungulates)

🐴
Horses (Equus caballus)

Primary intermediate and amplifying hosts during the 2014 Philippines outbreak.

  • Disease: Severe, fatal neurological syndrome
  • Transmission: Direct contact with sick horses
  • Risk: Handling/consumption of undercooked horse meat

🐕 5. Order Carnivora

🐕
Dogs (Canis lupus familiaris)

Frequently infected on pig farms during the Malaysian outbreak.

  • Lesions: Necrotizing lesions, pulmonary inflammation
  • Kidneys: Syncytial cell formation
  • Risk: Death of farm dogs was a critical risk factor for human transmission
🐱
Cats & Ferrets
  • Cats (Felis catus) Highly susceptible; severe disseminated endothelial syncytia and acute vasculopathy
  • Ferrets (Mustela putorius furo) Standard laboratory model; reliably induces severe systemic disease

🐹 6. Order Rodentia

🐹
Hamsters & Guinea Pigs
  • Golden/Syrian Hamsters Standard small-animal model; severe CNS lesions, systemic vasculopathy, lethal respiratory disease
  • Guinea Pigs Historically used; develop systemic vasculopathy and severe disease
🐭
Mice & Rats (Excluded)

Interestingly, clinical signs of disease are apparently absent in mice and rats during experimental infection, though the underlying biological mechanism for this resistance remains unknown.

(However, modified knockout mice models are sometimes utilized for tracking viral replication.)

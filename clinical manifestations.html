Subclinical / asymptomatic infection

A minority of infections produce no overt symptoms at all.

Silent infection is often undetected in surveillance, though its role in onward transmission remains a subject of study.

02

Prodromal phase

Non-specific, flu-like presentation: high-grade fever, severe headache, myalgia, sore throat, vomiting, dizziness.

Symptoms are easily mistaken for common viral illness in this window, which delays isolation and treatment before rapid progression to CNS involvement.

03

Encephalitic syndrome

  • Altered mental status, confusion, disorientation
  • Seizures, segmental myoclonus
  • Nystagmus, cerebellar ataxia, areflexia
  • Progression to coma
Death usually occurs within 6 days of symptom onset, most often driven by brainstem involvement.

Respiratory involvement — NiV-B lineage

Severe cough and atypical pneumonia, with rapid progression to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).

Prominent in the Bangladesh lineage but historically absent in Malaysian cohorts — a key clinical distinction between strains.

05

Case fatality rate

Outcome depends heavily on strain and on access to supportive care.

NiV-B carries the higher overall fatality rate (~75%) versus NiV-M (40–50%). Timely, well-resourced supportive care measurably improves survival odds.

06

Sequelae & relapsed / late-onset encephalitis

Survivors face lasting neurological risk: chronic fatigue, focal nerve palsies, myoclonus, depression. Pediatric survivors may show nightmares, violent outbursts, and other psychiatric effects.

Late-onset encephalitis can surface months or even years later — in one documented case, 11 years post-infection — attributed to viral persistence in brain tissue.

What is Nipah Virus (NiV)?

Nipah virus (NiV) is an emerging, highly pathogenic zoonotic virus that causes severe and often fatal neurological and respiratory diseases in both humans and animals. Due to its high virulence, extreme lethality, and the lack of widely approved vaccines or therapeutics for human use, it is classified globally as a Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4) agent.

NiV is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus belonging to the genus Henipavirus within the family Paramyxoviridae . It is closely related to the Hendra virus (HeV). Its natural reservoirs are fruit bats of the genus Pteropus (commonly known as flying foxes), which carry and shed the virus without showing clinical symptoms of disease.


The History and Outbreak Dynamics

Malaysia & Singapore

  • The First Outbreak (Malaysia & Singapore, 1998–1999): Nipah virus was first identified during an outbreak among pig farmers in September 1998 in Perak, Malaysia. The disease was initially misdiagnosed as Japanese encephalitis (JE), which delayed early containment efforts. In March 1999, scientists successfully isolated the virus from the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of a patient from Sungai Nipah (Nipah River Village), giving the virus its name.

This outbreak was driven by zoonotic transmission: fruit bats attracted to orchards near pig enclosures contaminated fruit and pigsties, introducing the virus to a naïve pig population. Pigs served as highly efficient intermediate and amplifying hosts, spreading the virus to humans via close physical contact. The Malaysian outbreak resulted in 283 symptomatic human cases and 109 deaths. It also spread to Singapore in March 1999, infecting 11 abattoir workers (with 1 death) who handled imported Malaysian pigs.

Bangladesh

Bangladesh (2001–Present): Since 2001, seasonal outbreaks have occurred nearly annually in central and northwestern Bangladesh (the "Nipah belt"). In contrast to Malaysia, the transmission here bypasses pigs entirely: humans contract the virus directly from bats by consuming raw date palm sap contaminated with bat saliva, urine, or feces. Interhuman transmission (via respiratory droplets and close contact) is highly prevalent in Bangladeshi outbreaks, which feature severe respiratory distress and a much higher case fatality rate of around 75%.

India

India (2001, 2007, 2018, 2023): India has experienced several outbreaks, beginning in West Bengal (Siliguri in 2001 and Nadia in 2007) and later in Kerala (2018, 2023). Like Bangladesh, these outbreaks are characterized by strong human-to-human and nosocomial (hospital-acquired) transmission, particularly affecting healthcare workers and family caregivers.

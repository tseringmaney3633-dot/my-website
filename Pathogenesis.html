Nipah virus · Clinical mechanism

How the virus moves through the body

From receptor binding to endothelial collapse — the cellular chain of events that turns a respiratory infection into vasculitis and encephalitis.

4 key stages 📄 4 notes 🎬 1 video

Receptor binding 01

  • The viral attachment protein (G) binds host cell surface receptors Ephrin‑B2 and Ephrin‑B3.
  • Both receptors are highly conserved across mammalian species, which helps explain Nipah's broad host range.
  • Ephrin‑B2 is expressed on arterial endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and bronchial epithelial cells.
  • Ephrin‑B3 is expressed predominantly in the central nervous system, including neurons.

Systemic dissemination 02

  • Entry occurs via the oro-nasal route, with initial replication in upper and lower respiratory epithelium and lymphoid tissue.
  • The virus then hitches a ride on circulating leukocytes, entering the bloodstream and producing systemic viraemia with widespread endothelial infection.

Syncytia formation 03

  • A hallmark of Nipah pathology is cell‑cell fusion between infected endothelial cells and adjacent uninfected cells.
  • This fusion, driven by the F and G glycoproteins, produces multinucleated giant syncytial cells — a distinctive marker seen on histopathology.

Histopathology 04

  • Endothelial damage triggers widespread small‑vessel vasculitis, thrombosis, ischemia, and parenchymal necrosis.
  • This primarily affects the brain stem, cerebral cortex, lungs, spleen, and kidneys.
  • Lungs frequently show severe alveolar hemorrhage and pulmonary edema, with necrotizing vasculitis progressing to atypical pneumonia.

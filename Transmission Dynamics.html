Transmission Dynamics

Two spillover routes out of one bat reservoir — and the strain that, unlike its cousin, didn't stop at the first person it infected.

Pteropus giganteus, the Indian flying fox — natural reservoir of Nipah virus
01 — Introduction

A single virus, telling two different stories

Nipah virus was first identified in 1998 during an outbreak among pig farmers in Malaysia, and it takes its name from Kampung Sungai Nipah, the village where it was traced. It belongs to the genus Henipavirus, and its natural reservoir is the fruit bat genus Pteropus, commonly known as flying foxes, which carry the virus without becoming sick themselves.

What makes Nipah worth tracking closely isn't just its severity — infection can progress from fever to fatal encephalitis — but the fact that it doesn't spread the same way everywhere. The Malaysian strain, NiV‑M, moved from bats into pigs and then into the people who handled them, largely stopping there. The Bangladesh and India strain, NiV‑B, skips the intermediate animal entirely, passing from bats straight to people through contaminated food, and then — critically — from those people to others.

That second detail is what separates a contained agricultural outbreak from a hospital-and-household chain of transmission. Understanding both routes, covered in the two sections below, is the difference between a spillover event and a spreading one.

02 — Zoonotic spillover

Two strains, two very different first steps

Before Nipah ever passes between people, it has to leave its animal reservoir. The route it takes shapes everything downstream — including how easily it goes on to spread further.

NiV‑M · Malaysia

Through the pig barn

Sus domesticus — intermediate amplifying host
Fruit bat → Infected pigs → Farm worker

Transmission occurred via direct contact with the respiratory secretions, body fluids, or tissue of infected pigs — an occupational exposure concentrated among people who worked closely with livestock.

Downstream effect: human‑to‑human spread from this route was extremely rare.
NiV‑B · Bangladesh & India

Through the sap pot

Pteropus spp. — direct reservoir contact
Fruit bat → Date palm sap → Person

Transmission occurs directly from bats to humans, typically through raw date palm sap harvested overnight. Bats lick the sap stream as it collects, contaminating the pot with saliva, urine, or feces. Eating fruit a bat has already bitten into carries the same risk.

Downstream effect: this route seeds the chains of person‑to‑person spread described below.
03 — Human‑to‑human
½

Roughly half of all NiV‑B cases in Bangladesh and India are acquired from another infected person, not from an animal or contaminated food. In Malaysian outbreaks, this mode of spread was a rare exception — in South Asia, it's a defining feature.

01

Droplets & aerosols

Close contact with respiratory droplets, saliva, or urine from an infected person.

02

Super‑spreaders

Critically ill patients over 25 with severe respiratory involvement carry a high viral load in their secretions and act as the primary drivers of hospital and community outbreaks.

03

Healthcare & corpse contact

Nosocomial spread to staff and visitors is common without proper PPE. Handling bodies of the deceased during traditional funerals is also high‑risk.
